An Excavation

The Layers

Every city I've lived in deposited a stratum. Six cities, six layers, one person. This is what digging looks like when you are the site.

Scroll to excavate ↓
Layer I

Bombay

Where it began
Foundation
"Density is not a problem to solve. It's a condition to design for."

I grew up in a city that never stops being built. Not metaphorically. Literally. Every monsoon season the cranes would multiply. Scaffolding made of bamboo and rope rose overnight on buildings that hadn't been designed so much as willed into existence.

Bombay taught me that the best architecture doesn't come from a clean sheet of paper. It comes from constraints. From the space between things. The gap between one structure and the next. The alley that becomes a living room, a kitchen, a workshop.

I learned to see design not as something you impose on the world, but as something you discover already there. Waiting.

Layer II

Bournemouth

Studied Architecture at AUB
Displacement
"The first tool is not a pen. It's the ability to listen."

I left Bombay for Bournemouth. The shift was geological. A city of millions to a coastal town. The streets stayed clean. The buses ran on time. Everything was measured, planned, permitted. I felt like I'd stepped into a diorama.

In the studio, my classmates drew differently than me. Their lines were tentative, searching. Mine were sure, maybe too sure. Bombay had taught me to commit fast because the city doesn't wait for your second draft. Here, deliberation was the craft.

I struggled with that. But somewhere in that quiet, I started to hear my own thinking. Bournemouth taught me hesitation. And hesitation, I learned, is not weakness. It's the space where a better answer forms.

Layer III

Beijing

Architectural Assistant · Co-founded De-facto
Expansion
"Design at the speed of a city being built."

Beijing broke my sense of scale. In Bournemouth, a five-storey building was a project. In Beijing, entire districts rose while you blinked. I watched a 500-metre axis go from dust to steel in months. Not a building. A city block.

I worked across practices and co-founded De-facto, a small collective experimenting with 3D printing and digital fabrication. We applied architectural thinking to fashion, food, installations. The walls between disciplines felt arbitrary. The interesting work was in the gaps.

Beijing taught me ambition. But more than that, it taught me that ambition without craft is just noise. And one thing more, something I wouldn't fully understand for another decade:

  • Computation could generate more than buildings. It could generate patterns.
  • And patterns, once you learn to read them, look a lot like intelligence.
Layer IV

Barcelona

IAAC · Computational Design & Advanced Fabrication
Refinement
"Technology is nothing without craft."

Beijing fed me speed. Barcelona fed me patience.

I walked the Eixample. Cerdà's grid. Every block chamfered at the corner, a city designed for light and air before anyone knew to ask for it. I sat in Gaudí's basement at the Sagrada Família, watching craftsmen carve stone using the same geometries his parametric models had described a century earlier.

Here was the lesson that rewired me: Gaudí's hanging chain models, inverted catenary arches calculated with sandbags, were algorithms. He was doing parametric design with gravity as his processor. The technology changed. The thinking hadn't.

Barcelona taught me that craft is not the enemy of computation. It is computation's predecessor. And its conscience.

Layer V

Hong Kong

Enzyme APD · UNStudio · The Shift
Compression
"Constraints are the highest form of creativity."

Hong Kong is Bombay turned vertical and intensified.

Seven million people. 1,100 square kilometres. Ninety per cent of it mountain and water. The city doesn't expand, it intensifies. Every square inch is negotiated, layered, re-used.

This was where computation became strategy for me. When the site is tight, you need every millimetre to earn its keep. The only way to optimise at that resolution is to let the machine help you see what the eye can't.

I worked at Enzyme APD, then UNStudio. Computational Designer, Architectural Designer, Design Technology Specialist. The titles stacked like the city itself. And somewhere in that stacking, the shift happened: from architect who uses technology to technologist who understands architecture.

Layer VI

London

Benoy · Head of Technology · Global Head of Design AI & Technology
Emergence
"Each city had deposited a layer. London was where they started speaking to each other."

London is where it all converged. I arrived as a computational designer. I became a design technologist. Then a strategist. Then a leader. The titles matter less than what was happening underneath: each city I'd lived in had deposited a layer, and London was the place where those layers started speaking to each other.

At Benoy, I built a design technology function from scratch. BIM and computational design teams. Infrastructure, sustainability tech, automation pipelines, custom tools. Then with IT, enterprise AI platforms now used across the practice globally.

And somewhere in that process, I realised I wasn't practicing architecture anymore. I was practicing something else. Something I didn't have a name for yet.

Present Day

What I’m building now

For years I thought I was designing buildings. Then I thought I was designing the tools for designing buildings. Then I thought I was designing the teams that build the tools.

I was wrong all three times.

What I've actually been doing, across six cities and two decades, is training a mind to see patterns. To recognise the shape of a problem before anyone has drawn it. To hold complexity without collapsing under it. To know that the answer is almost never in the brief. It's in the layer underneath.

Most firms treat AI as a technology problem. It isn't. It's a practice problem. The machine doesn't create intelligence. It helps us excavate it. The intelligence was always there, waiting in the constraints, in the density, in the space between one layer and the next.

I used to think I was becoming an architect.
Then a technologist. Then a strategist. Then a leader.

But I was becoming someone who could see the whole thing at once. The foundation and the roof. The code and the craft. The bamboo scaffolding and the neural network.

I was becoming someone who could build anything.
Because I knew what was underneath.
